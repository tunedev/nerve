# E3.3 — streamrotatefs

A mount of rotating logs. A write appends to a name; once what is there reaches the
mount's segment size it is sealed under a numbered sibling and the name starts empty
again. The ticket's acceptance is one sentence — rotation is observable through
`read_dir` — and it is `ReadDir` that carries it: a segment appears in a listing as it
is sealed and leaves it as retention drops it.

```
$ go test ./internal/adapters/outbound/streamrotatefs/ -run TestConformance -v | grep -cE '^=== RUN   TestConformance/[^/]+$'
38
$ go test -race ./internal/adapters/outbound/streamrotatefs/ -run 'TestRotation|TestRetention|TestAWrite|TestConcurrent|TestAMount' -v | grep '^--- '
--- PASS: TestRotationIsObservableThroughReadDir (0.00s)
--- PASS: TestRetentionDropsTheOldestSegmentFromTheListing (0.00s)
--- PASS: TestAWriteIsNeverSplitAcrossSegments (0.00s)
--- PASS: TestAMountStaysBounded (0.00s)
--- PASS: TestConcurrentWritersRotateOneLogSafely (0.00s)
```

## What the pattern was

**The same bound as `streamfs`, made visible.** Both kinds exist so that a process
writing forever occupies a fixed amount. `streamfs` drops the oldest bytes inside a
ring, and nothing in the port says which bytes went — a follower finds out through
`ErrOverrun` and a reader of the whole window never finds out at all. Here the unit of
discard is a whole segment and it is a name leaving a listing, so what the mount threw
away is something a caller can enumerate. That is the whole difference between the two
kinds, and it is why the ticket's acceptance is about `read_dir` rather than about
reading.

**Every name in the mount is a rotating log, sealed segments included.** There is no
name shape that means "segment" and no second kind of entry. `app` seals to `app.1`,
and `app.1` is from then on an ordinary name of the mount: readable, listable,
removable, and itself a log that would rotate to `app.1.1` if anyone wrote enough to
it. Which entries are `app`'s history is `app`'s own record, not something a reader
infers from a name. Retention reads that record.

**Uniform `Caps{AppendOnly, ObjectStore}` at every path.** The tempting design was to
claim `ReadOnly` on sealed segments — capability varying inside one mount is the
architecture's centrepiece, and `queuefs` already does it. It is wrong here, and the
descriptor says why: read-only means *every* mutating verb fails with
`fs.ErrPermission`, `Remove` included. A mount whose segments could never be removed
could never be cleared, and retention would be the only thing on earth allowed to
delete them. The descriptor was not worth the mount becoming ungovernable.

**One mount-wide lock, not one per log.** `streamfs` gives each stream its own lock so
a writer to one never waits on a reader of another. That does not transfer: every
write here may rotate, and rotation adds and removes names, so a per-log lock would
have to take the map lock underneath it to finish the write it started. Tenet 4's
"a mutex is often the honest answer" is the answer.

## What surprised me

**Sealing has to pass over a name a caller already took.** The mount is append-only,
which means nothing replaces anything — and rotation writes to a name it chose rather
than one a caller gave it. If a caller has written to `app.1`, sealing `app` there
would replace their bytes, and the descriptor the kind claims at that very path says
that cannot happen. So the sequence number advances until it finds a free name. The
counter still only goes up, so a name a segment had is never handed to another.

Notably this is not a defensive check bolted on: it is the append-only claim being
honoured at the one place where the mount, rather than a caller, picks a name.

**A write larger than a segment makes a segment larger than the size.** The alternative
is cutting inside the write, and a rotated log whose records can be split across two
files is not worth reading. So the whole write lands and only then is the segment
sealed — `size` is the point at which a segment stops growing, not a cap on how large
it gets. The conformance suite never noticed either way; it is asserted here.

**The conformance suite ran green the first time, and that was not luck.** `kvfs` and
`streamfs` had already established the flat keyspace with the key-over-prefix listing
rule, and this kind is a third mount of exactly that shape. Thirty-eight of the suite's
seventy-one cases run, and the thirty-three that skip are the ones that would have
asked a log to be replaced, cut, walked or consumed.

## What I would do differently

Reach for the wrapper before writing the map. The first design had `streamrotatefs`
holding a `vfs.FileSystem` for its keyspace and adding rotation on `Write`, which
composes existing pieces and would have been half the code. It does not survive
concurrency: append-then-rotate through a generic port is read-modify-write across
three calls, and concurrent writers lose appends. `kvfs` says as much in its own doc —
its `Chmod` and `Rename` are not atomic across the port, because the port has no
transaction to make them one. The port is the wrong seam for anything that has to be
atomic, and that is worth knowing before drawing the boxes rather than after.

## What I still do not understand

Whether the segment numbers should sort. `ReadDir` is sorted by name, so `app.10`
lands before `app.2`, and a caller wanting the segments in the order they were sealed
reads the number rather than trusting the listing. Zero-padding fixes the ordering and
buys a ceiling on how many rotations a log may ever have, which is a worse trade for a
mount meant to run unattended. Sorting by `ModTime` works today and would stop working
the moment two segments seal inside one clock tick.

Whether `Remove` of a log should take its segments with it. It does not: they are
entries of their own and `Remove` deletes the path it was given. That leaves a removed
log's history in the mount with nothing left that knows it is history, so retention
will never drop it and only `RemoveAll` of the prefix above will. The alternative is a
`Remove` that deletes names the caller did not name, which is worse in a different
way. Neither is obviously right, and no caller has met it yet.

Whether this kind and `streamfs` should be one kind with two policies. They differ in
one decision — whether the discarded unit is a byte or a segment — and share a
keyspace, a descriptor set and most of a file. Two kinds is the honest answer while
only `streamfs` can be followed; if `streamrotatefs` ever grows a `Streamer` that hops
segment boundaries, that answer is worth revisiting.
