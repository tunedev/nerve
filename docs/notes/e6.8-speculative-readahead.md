# E6.8 — Speculative readahead

A streaming read now has a fetch of its own running in front of it. `OpenRead`
starts a goroutine that pulls the plugin's stream into a queue holding up to
`readahead-window` bytes, and the caller's `Read` is served out of that queue.
It is speculative: those bytes are bytes nobody has asked for, and a caller that
stops early paid for at most a window of them.

`Registry.Build` puts it on every mount, so what E6 reads through it gets it
without asking: the digest pass that finalises a resource, and the content a
signed download redeems.

The acceptance is "prefetch measurably reduces first-read latency, or is
removed". Over a source costing 2ms a piece, with a caller costing 2ms a piece:

```
$ go test ./internal/core/vfs/ -run '^$' -bench 'LatentSource|FirstRead' -benchtime 50x
BenchmarkStreamOverALatentSource/off-32           50    70783938 ns/op
BenchmarkStreamOverALatentSource/one-fetch-32     50    36836794 ns/op
BenchmarkStreamOverALatentSource/default-32       50    36827629 ns/op
BenchmarkFirstReadAfterOpen/off-32                50     2143970 ns/op
BenchmarkFirstReadAfterOpen/default-32            50        8028 ns/op
```

First read 2.14ms to 0.008ms, and a 512KiB stream 70.8ms to 36.8ms. It stays.

## What the pattern was

`WithReadahead` is the sixth decorator in `internal/core/vfs` and the first that
changes only one verb. The other five — bound, breaker, retry, recorder,
observer — each have something to say about all ten; this one forwards nine of
them and the two accessors untouched, and wraps only what `AsStreamer` hands
back. Writing the forwarding out by hand rather than embedding `FileSystem`
keeps it the same shape as its neighbours, and keeps a verb added to the port a
compile error here rather than a silent pass-through.

The queue is a buffered channel of pieces and the stopping rule is the whole
design:

- `Close` closes the source first, which is what ends a pull already in flight,
  and then drains the queue. Draining is what guarantees the fetch can always
  land its last send, so it always reaches its own return and nothing outlives
  the `Close`.
- Why the source stopped travels *in* the queue, behind the bytes, rather than
  beside them. A source that failed partway hands over what it moved before it
  reports the failure.

## What surprised me

**Where the wrapper goes in the stack decides whether it breaks the idle
bound.** The obvious place was next to the other five, above `WithTimeout`. That
is wrong, and quietly. `WithTimeout` splits a stream in two — a deadline on the
open and an idle bound on the transfer — and the idle bound is restarted by
every read that moves a byte *through it*. Put the fetch above it and the reads
it counts are the fetch's, not the caller's; once the queue is full the fetch
stops pulling, no byte crosses the bound, and the transfer of a caller that is
merely slower than the window is deep gets ended for a far end that was
answering perfectly well. Under the bound, the reads the idle watch counts are
the caller's own drains of the queue, and the semantics are the ones that were
there before. `Registry.Build` therefore puts it innermost, against the plugin.

**A queue's job cannot be read ahead of, and the mount already knew that.** What
the queue holds when a caller closes is dropped — that is the cost of reading
ahead. On a path where a read consumes, dropping it loses work nobody ever
received. The fix needed no knowledge of what a queue is: `Capabilities(path)`
already answers `ReadDestructive`, and `OpenRead` hands back the plugin's stream
bare when it does. It is the same rule `WithWriteObserver` applies for the same
reason, reached the same way.

**Depth past one fetch bought nothing here, and that is the benchmark being
honest rather than the window being wrong.** `one-fetch` and the 256KiB default
are within noise of each other, because the harness's source costs exactly the
same per piece every time. One piece in hand is enough to cover a caller when
the next piece always takes the same 2ms. A window is what covers a source whose
pieces are *uneven* — a bucket that stalls on one range request — and the
benchmark has no jitter in it to show that.

## What I would do differently

The guard on the acceptance is a behavioural test, not a timing one: the suite
asserts that the source has been pulled while the caller has read nothing, and
that with the window off nothing reaches the source until the caller asks. The
numbers live in benchmarks, which nothing fails on. A regression that made the
fetch synchronous again would be caught; one that merely made it slower would
not.

## What I still do not understand

Whether 256KiB is the right default across the kinds this actually wraps. It is
eight fetches, chosen because it is small enough to throw away and large enough
to cover a caller, and nothing here has measured it against a real bucket over a
real network. The setting exists so that answer can be found without a
recompile, and `readahead-window: 0` is the whole of removing it.
